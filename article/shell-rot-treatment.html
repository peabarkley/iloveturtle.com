Shell Rot in Turtles: Causes, Treatment & Prevention

By iLoveTurtle Editorial Team Published September 17, 2026 11 min read
Last updated: September 17, 2026

Shell rot is one of the most common health problems in captive turtles — and one of the most misunderstood. Beginners often mistake it for a cosmetic issue or a normal shell change, and by the time they realize something is wrong, the infection has already eaten into the bone.

This guide covers what shell rot actually is, how to tell bacterial from fungal infection, how to treat early cases at home, when to see a vet, and how to prevent it from ever happening again.

Medical note: This guide is for information only and does not replace veterinary examination. If the affected area is soft, crumbly, or has exposed bone, or if your turtle is lethargic or refusing food, contact an exotic animal vet immediately.

What Shell Rot Actually Is

"Shell rot" is a general term for any bacterial or fungal infection that damages the shell. In veterinary medicine, the condition is called ulcerative shell disease when the outer keratin layer is involved, and osteomyelitis when the infection has reached the bone underneath.

The shell is not a dead, hard covering. It is living bone covered with keratin scutes, and it has blood vessels and nerve endings. An infection that enters the shell can spread into the turtle's bloodstream — which is why shell rot is a medical issue, not a cosmetic one.

Shell rot does not heal on its own. It is a progressive infection, and without treatment it will spread across the shell and eventually into the body.

Bacterial vs Fungal Shell Rot

Both types look similar to the naked eye, and they often occur together. The distinction matters because the treatment is different.

Bacterial Shell Rot Fungal Shell Rot
Appearance Soft, pitted, dark or reddish patches White, grey, or yellowish patches that look powdery
Smell Foul, rotting odor Musty, earthy odor
Common cause Dirty water, injury, gram-negative bacteria Chronic moisture, poor ventilation, low temperature
Treatment Antibiotic ointments; systemic antibiotics for deep cases Antifungal creams; systemic antifungals for deep cases

In practice, both are present in most cases. A vet can culture the affected tissue to identify the specific organism, but for home treatment of early-stage shell rot, a broad-spectrum approach (povidone-iodine plus silver sulfadiazine) handles both.

Symptoms and Stages

Stage 1 — Superficial. Small white, grey, or yellowish patches on the shell surface. The shell still feels hard. There may be a slight odor. This stage responds well to home treatment.

Stage 2 — Moderate. The affected area becomes soft, pitted, or crumbly. The surface may flake or peel. The odor becomes stronger. Home treatment can still work, but a vet visit is recommended.

Stage 3 — Severe. The keratin layer is gone, exposing raw tissue or bone. There may be discharge, bleeding, or a visible defect in the shell. The turtle may be lethargic or refusing food. This requires immediate veterinary care and systemic antibiotics.

Other warning signs that accompany shell rot:

  • Lethargy and reduced appetite
  • Reluctance to bask or, conversely, constant basking
  • Swollen limbs or joints (a sign the infection has spread)
  • Weight loss
  • Sunken eyes or dehydration

Causes: The Husbandry Problem

Shell rot is almost always a symptom of poor husbandry. The pathogens that cause it are common in aquariums and on turtle skin, but they only cause disease when the turtle's defenses are compromised.

Primary causes:

  • Dirty water. High bacterial loads overwhelm the shell's natural defenses. This is the number one cause in captivity.
  • Insufficient basking. Turtles must be able to leave the water and dry the shell completely. If the basking platform is too small, too slippery, or too cold, the shell stays wet — and wet shell is where rot starts.
  • Low water temperature. Cold water suppresses the immune system. Water below 22°C for extended periods is a risk factor.
  • Inadequate UVB. Without UVB, turtles cannot properly maintain shell health, and their immune function is compromised.
  • Shell injury. Any crack, chip, or scrape in the shell creates an entry point for infection.
  • Chronic moisture under the shell. Poor tank ventilation or constant high humidity without drying opportunities.

If you treat shell rot without fixing the underlying cause, it will come back. Every time.

Home Treatment for Early Cases

Early-stage, superficial shell rot can be treated at home. But home treatment only works if you also fix the environment.

Step 1: Assess the damage. Press gently on the affected area with a clean finger. If the shell is still hard, this is likely Stage 1 and can be treated at home. If it is soft, crumbly, or has exposed tissue, see a vet.

Step 2: Clean the tank. Do a 100% water change, clean the filter, siphon the substrate, and scrub the tank walls. This removes the bacterial load that started the problem.

Step 3: Dry-dock the turtle. Move the turtle to a warm, dry container with a basking lamp and UVB. Provide a shallow water dish for drinking and short supervised swims. The turtle should spend 20 to 22 hours per day dry-docked during treatment.

Step 4: Clean the affected area. Use a soft toothbrush and diluted povidone-iodine (Betadine) or chlorhexidine solution to gently scrub the affected area. Do not scrub hard — you can damage healthy tissue. Rinse with clean water.

Step 5: Apply medication. Apply a thin layer of silver sulfadiazine cream (Silvadene) or a triple-antibiotic ointment. Silver sulfadiazine is preferred because it covers both bacteria and fungi.

Step 6: Repeat daily. Clean and reapply medication once per day. Progress should be visible within 1 to 2 weeks.

Step 7: Fix the environment. Before the turtle returns to the main tank, ensure:

  • Water temperature is 24 to 28°C
  • Basking platform reaches 32 to 35°C
  • UVB bulb is fresh (replaced within the last 6 to 9 months)
  • Filter is rated for at least 2x the tank volume
  • Water changes are done weekly

Do not use:

  • Hydrogen peroxide (damages healthy tissue)
  • Alcohol (painful and damaging)
  • Bleach (toxic even at low concentrations)
  • Vaseline or oil-based ointments (trap moisture and make rot worse)

When to See a Vet

Contact a reptile veterinarian if any of the following apply:

  • The affected area is soft, crumbly, or has exposed bone.
  • There is a strong, foul odor.
  • The shell has visible pits, holes, or deep defects.
  • The turtle is lethargic, refusing food, or showing other signs of illness.
  • The condition has not improved after 1 to 2 weeks of home treatment.
  • The affected area is larger than a small patch, or covers more than one scute.

A vet will typically:

  • Examine the shell and assess the depth of the infection.
  • Take a culture swab to identify the specific bacteria or fungus.
  • Prescribe systemic antibiotics or antifungals (oral or injectable).
  • Debride the affected tissue, which may involve removing dead or infected scutes.
  • Recommend ongoing wound care and environmental corrections.

Severe shell rot can take months to heal. The bone underneath the shell must regenerate, which is a slow process. During this time, the turtle should stay in a clean, warm, dry-docked environment with daily short swims.

Prevention: Fix the Environment

Shell rot is a preventable disease. The following steps will prevent it in virtually every case:

  • Keep the water clean. Weekly 25–50% water changes, a filter rated for 2x the tank volume, and prompt removal of uneaten food.
  • Provide a proper basking area. Large enough for the turtle to fully dry off, stable, non-slip, and positioned under a heat lamp and UVB bulb.
  • Maintain correct water temperature. 24 to 28°C for most species.
  • Use a fresh UVB bulb. Replace every 6 to 12 months, even if it still glows.
  • Inspect the shell weekly. Look for changes in color, texture, or smell. Early detection is the easiest cure.
  • Address injuries immediately. Any crack, chip, or scrape should be cleaned and monitored.
  • Avoid overcrowding. Multiple turtles in a tank increase waste load and the risk of injury from bites.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does shell rot look like on a turtle?

Early shell rot appears as small white, grey, or yellowish patches on the shell. As it progresses, the affected areas become soft, pitted, or crumbly, and may emit a foul, musty smell. In severe cases, the outer keratin layer peels away to reveal underlying bone. Bacterial and fungal rot look similar and often occur together.

Can shell rot heal on its own?

No. Shell rot is a progressive infection that will not resolve without treatment. If caught early and the underlying husbandry problem is fixed, mild cases can be treated at home. Any case that has penetrated the keratin layer, or that covers more than a small area, requires veterinary treatment with antibiotics or antifungals.

What causes shell rot in turtles?

Shell rot is caused by bacteria or fungi that colonize damaged or chronically wet shell tissue. The most common underlying causes are dirty water, insufficient basking opportunities, injury to the shell, and low water temperature. Without a dry basking area, the shell never dries out, and pathogens thrive in the moist environment.

How do I treat early shell rot at home?

For early, superficial shell rot: gently clean the affected area with a soft toothbrush and diluted povidone-iodine, dry the shell thoroughly, apply an over-the-counter antibiotic ointment or silver sulfadiazine cream, and keep the turtle dry-docked for 20 to 22 hours a day with short supervised swims. Fix the underlying husbandry issue immediately.

When should I take my turtle to a vet for shell rot?

See a vet if: the affected area is soft, crumbly, or has an odor, the shell has deep pits or exposed bone, the turtle is lethargic, refusing food, or has other signs of illness, or the condition does not improve within 1 to 2 weeks of home treatment. Severe shell rot requires systemic antibiotics or antifungals and possibly surgical debridement.

Can I catch shell rot from my turtle?

Shell rot itself is not typically transmitted to humans, but turtles commonly carry Salmonella and other bacteria on their skin and shells. Always wash your hands thoroughly with soap after handling the turtle or cleaning its tank. Do not clean turtle equipment in a kitchen sink or bathtub used by humans.

Final Thoughts

Shell rot is serious, but it is also preventable. In the vast majority of cases, it starts with dirty water or inadequate basking — two problems that are entirely within the keeper's control. Fix the environment, inspect the shell regularly, and treat any early signs immediately. A turtle with proper husbandry will almost never develop shell rot in the first place.
