Turtle Eye Infection: Symptoms & Home Treatment

By iLoveTurtle Editorial Team Published September 17, 2026 8 min read
Last updated: September 17, 2026

Swollen, closed eyes are one of the most common health problems in captive turtles — and one of the most distressing to watch. A turtle that cannot open its eyes will often stop eating, become lethargic, and decline quickly if the underlying cause is not addressed.

The good news: most turtle eye infections are treatable at home, especially when caught early. The key is to identify the cause and correct it, not just treat the symptom.

Medical note: This guide is for information only and does not replace veterinary examination. If the eye does not improve within 3–5 days, if the turtle stops eating, or if there is thick discharge or a cloudy cornea, contact an exotic animal vet.

Symptoms to Look For

Turtle eye problems usually start subtly and get worse over days or weeks. Watch for:

  • Swollen eyelids. The eyes look puffy, and the turtle may keep them closed most or all of the time.
  • Cloudy or white cornea. The clear part of the eye becomes opaque or milky.
  • Discharge. Clear, white, or pus-like fluid around the eye.
  • Rubbing the eyes. The turtle uses its front claws to paw at its face.
  • Refusal to open eyes. Even when awake, the turtle keeps both eyes shut.
  • Loss of appetite. Often follows eye closure, because the turtle cannot see food.
  • Lethargy and basking more than usual. Late-stage signs that the condition has been going on for a while.

Any one of these signs is enough reason to act. Do not wait for the condition to become severe — eye infections that are treated early usually resolve quickly.

Common Causes

1. Vitamin A deficiency (hypovitaminosis A). This is the most common cause of swollen eyes in turtles. It occurs when the diet lacks vitamin-A-rich foods, or when the turtle cannot absorb vitamin A properly due to poor husbandry. Vitamin A deficiency also affects the skin, respiratory tract, and kidneys, so eye problems are often the first visible sign of a broader nutritional issue.

2. Dirty water. High bacterial loads in the water can cause conjunctivitis and secondary infections. This is especially common when filtration is inadequate or water changes are infrequent.

3. Poor water quality. High ammonia or nitrite levels irritate the eyes and make infection more likely.

4. Injury. A scratch from tank decor, a bite from a tank mate, or a foreign object in the eye can introduce bacteria.

5. Overly intense or direct lighting. Some turtles develop eye irritation from UVB bulbs mounted too close, or from bright lights pointed directly at the eyes.

6. Respiratory infection. Eye discharge can be a symptom of a broader respiratory infection. If the turtle also has nasal discharge, open-mouth breathing, or floating tilted, treat for respiratory infection — see our respiratory infection guide.

Home Treatment

For early, mild cases — where the eye is swollen but not ulcerated and the turtle is still eating — home treatment can work well.

Step 1: Correct the diet. This is the most important step. Add vitamin-A-rich foods:

  • Grated carrot
  • Sweet potato (cooked)
  • Red bell pepper (many turtles are attracted to the red color)
  • Dandelion greens
  • Kale, collard greens, mustard greens

See our complete diet guide for a full list of vitamin-A-rich foods.

Step 2: Clean the tank and filter. Do a complete water change, clean the filter, and siphon the substrate. High bacterial load in the water is a major contributing factor.

Step 3: Raise the water temperature. Bring the water to 27–29°C (80–85°F). Stable warmth supports the immune system and speeds healing.

Step 4: Flush the eye. Use a saline eyewash or sterile saline solution. Gently flush the eye two to three times daily. Saline is available at any pharmacy. This removes discharge and keeps the eye moist.

Step 5: Apply eye medication. For mild cases, a reptile-specific eye drop or a preservative-free lubricating drop is enough. For more serious cases, a vet may prescribe an antibiotic eye drop. See the note below about human eye drops.

Step 6: Dry-dock the turtle. Move the turtle to a warm, dry container with a basking lamp and UVB. Provide a shallow water dish for drinking and short supervised swims. Dry-docking keeps the eye from being constantly wet, which helps it heal.

Step 7: Monitor for improvement. The eye should improve within 3 to 5 days. If it does not, or if the turtle stops eating, see a vet.

About human eye drops: Never use eye drops that contain steroids (like hydrocortisone) — they can worsen an infection. Saline and preservative-free lubricating drops are safe. Antibiotic eye drops should only be used under veterinary guidance.

When to See a Vet

Contact a reptile veterinarian if:

  • The eye does not improve within 3 to 5 days of home treatment.
  • The turtle stops eating.
  • There is thick, colored, or bloody discharge.
  • The cornea is cloudy, ulcerated, or has a visible defect.
  • The turtle is lethargic or showing other signs of illness.
  • Both eyes are affected and completely closed.

A vet will typically:

  • Examine the eye and assess the extent of the infection.
  • Take a swab for culture if bacterial infection is suspected.
  • Prescribe antibiotic or antifungal eye drops.
  • Administer a vitamin A injection if deficiency is diagnosed.
  • Recommend systemic antibiotics if the infection has spread.

Severe eye infections can lead to blindness and systemic infection if left untreated. Do not delay a vet visit if the symptoms are not improving.

Prevention

Turtle eye infections are almost entirely preventable with proper husbandry.

  • Feed a varied, vitamin-A-rich diet. Commercial pellets alone are not enough. Rotate in fresh vegetables and occasional fruit. See our diet guide.
  • Keep the water clean. Weekly 25–50% water changes, a filter rated for 2x the tank volume, and prompt removal of uneaten food.
  • Maintain proper water temperature. 24–28°C for most species.
  • Provide proper UVB. UVB supports immune function and overall health. See our UVB guide.
  • Mount lights correctly. UVB bulbs should be mounted at the recommended distance — not so close that they irritate the eyes.
  • Inspect the eyes weekly. Early detection makes treatment much easier.

Frequently Asked Questions

What causes swollen eyes in turtles?

The most common cause is vitamin A deficiency, often called hypovitaminosis A. This occurs when turtles are fed a poor-quality diet, typically a diet lacking in vitamin-A-rich foods. Other causes include bacterial infection from dirty water, poor water quality, and physical injury. In many cases, multiple factors are involved at the same time.

Can I treat a turtle eye infection at home?

Early, mild cases can often be treated at home. The first step is to correct the underlying cause: improve the diet to include vitamin-A-rich foods, clean the tank and filter, and raise the water temperature to 27–29°C. Apply a saline eyewash or a reptile-specific eye drop two to three times daily. If the eye does not improve within 3–5 days, or if the turtle stops eating, see a vet.

What vitamin A foods should I feed a turtle with eye problems?

Offer foods rich in vitamin A: grated carrot, sweet potato, red bell pepper, dandelion greens, and dark leafy greens like kale and collard greens. For turtles that are not eating, a vet can administer a vitamin A injection. Do not give oral vitamin A supplements without veterinary guidance — hypervitaminosis A is toxic.

When should I see a vet for a turtle eye infection?

See a vet if: the eye does not improve within 3–5 days of home treatment, the turtle stops eating, there is thick or bloody discharge, the cornea is cloudy or ulcerated, or the turtle is lethargic. Severe eye infections can lead to blindness and systemic infection if left untreated.

Can I use human eye drops on my turtle?

Do not use human eye drops that contain steroids (like hydrocortisone) — they can worsen an infection. Saline eyewash and preservative-free lubricating drops are safe for flushing. For medicated drops, use a reptile-specific product or one prescribed by your vet. Human antibiotic eye drops can be used in some cases, but only under veterinary guidance.

Final Thoughts

Turtle eye infections are common, but they are also treatable — and largely preventable. The most important step is to correct the underlying cause, not just treat the symptom. A vitamin-A-rich diet, clean water, and proper UVB will prevent the vast majority of eye problems from ever occurring.
