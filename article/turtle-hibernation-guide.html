Turtle Hibernation Guide: What, Why and How

By Sarah Whitfield March 20, 2026 11 min read
Last updated: March 20, 2026

Hibernation — more accurately called brumation in reptiles — is one of the most misunderstood topics in turtle keeping. Done correctly, it supports long-term health and natural hormonal cycles. Done wrong, it is one of the leading causes of death in captive temperate turtles.

This guide covers which turtles should hibernate, how to prepare them, the safe fridge method, and the warning signs that mean you must abort and warm the animal back up.

Before you start: never hibernate a sick, underweight, or first-year turtle. If you are not confident, overwintering with heat is always a safe alternative. A turtle does not suffer from skipping brumation, but it can die from a badly managed one.

Hibernation vs Brumation

Mammals hibernate. Reptiles brumate. The two are similar in effect — a state of greatly reduced metabolism triggered by cold — but the biology is different. Brumating turtles remain semi-responsive: they can still move slowly, drink, and even shift position in their substrate. They are not in true "sleep."

In practice, most keepers use the word "hibernation" for both. Throughout this guide, we use the terms interchangeably.

Which Turtles Hibernate and Which Do Not

Brumation is triggered by the turtle's natural climate, not by the keeper's preference. Species from temperate zones must have a cool winter to stay healthy long-term. Tropical species must not be cooled at all.

Species that should brumate (temperate):

  • Red-eared slider
  • Painted turtle
  • Musk turtle
  • Chinese pond turtle (grass turtle)
  • Hermann's tortoise
  • Russian tortoise
  • European pond turtle

Species that must NOT brumate (tropical):

  • Red-footed tortoise
  • Sulcata tortoise
  • Leopard tortoise
  • Yellow-footed tortoise
  • Most softshell turtles
  • Pig-nosed turtle

If your species is on the second list and you cool it down, you risk pneumonia and death. Keep tropical species at 26 to 30°C year-round with full UVB.

Pre-Hibernation Preparation (6 Weeks Out)

Preparation is the difference between a safe brumation and a fatal one. The turtle must enter winter with enough fat reserves to survive 8 to 16 weeks without food.

Pre-Hibernation Checklist

  • 6 weeks before: switch to a high-quality diet, increase portion size slightly, and confirm the turtle is gaining weight. Weigh weekly.
  • 4 weeks before: book a wellness check with a reptile vet. A fecal exam is recommended — intestinal parasites bloom during the metabolic slowdown.
  • 2 weeks before: stop feeding. Continue daily warm baths so the gut empties completely.
  • 1 week before: keep the turtle at normal temperature but with no food. If the turtle has not defecated by the end of this week, do not hibernate — the risk of gut fermentation is too high.
  • Day before: do a final weight check. Note any discrepancy in the log. Prepare the hibernation box and substrate.

Weighing matters. Write the pre-hibernation weight down and stick it on the fridge. Weight loss above 10% during brumation is a red flag.

The Fridge Method, Step by Step

Most experienced keepers use a dedicated refrigerator. It gives you precise control over temperature and keeps humidity consistent — two factors that are hard to manage outdoors in a changing climate.

Equipment:

  • A fridge that never holds food. Set it to 5 to 8°C.
  • A plastic box with a lid, ventilated with several small holes.
  • Substrate: damp sphagnum moss, coco coir, or paper towels.
  • A digital thermometer-hygrometer placed inside the box.
  • A separate small container of water to keep the humidity up (do not put the turtle in it).

Steps:

  1. Dampen the substrate until it holds together but does not drip.
  2. Place the turtle on top of the substrate, then cover it loosely with a handful of the same material.
  3. Put the box in the fridge, on a middle shelf — never on the top shelf, which is coldest.
  4. Close the door and check the thermometer after 30 minutes. Adjust the fridge setting until it holds 5 to 8°C.
  5. Check the box weekly: substrate moisture, temperature, and the turtle's condition.

If the substrate dries out, mist it lightly. If it becomes soaked, replace part of it — a wet turtle in cold conditions develops shell rot quickly.

Monitoring During Hibernation

Do not open the fridge every day. Each opening raises the temperature and wakes the turtle slightly, burning fat it cannot afford to lose. Weekly checks are enough.

  • Temperature: 5 to 8°C. Never above 12°C, never below 3°C.
  • Humidity: 70 to 90%. If the box feels dry, mist the substrate.
  • Weight: weigh monthly. Loss above 10% of pre-hibernation weight is an emergency.
  • Shell condition: check for soft spots, discoloration, or odor.
  • Eyes and nose: sunken eyes or any discharge means dehydration or infection.

Warning Signs: When to Abort

Stop hibernation and warm the turtle gradually if you see any of the following:

  • Weight loss above 10% of the starting weight.
  • Sunken eyes, dry skin, or a soft shell.
  • Any nasal discharge, bubbles, or open-mouth breathing.
  • The turtle is awake and moving regularly, not semi-dormant.
  • The fridge temperature has risen above 12°C for more than a few hours.

To abort safely, move the box to a 15°C room for 24 hours, then to 20°C for another 24 hours, then to normal housing temperature. Do not put the turtle in warm water or under a hot lamp immediately — rapid warming can cause shock.

Waking Up Safely

At the end of the brumation period, warm the turtle gradually:

  1. Move the box to a 15°C room for 24 hours.
  2. Move to 20°C for another 24 hours.
  3. Set up a normal tank at 25 to 28°C with a basking spot of 32°C and a UVB lamp.
  4. Soak the turtle in shallow, room-temperature water for 20 minutes to rehydrate.
  5. Offer a small meal after 24 to 48 hours. If the turtle refuses, wait another day and try again.
  6. Weigh the turtle on day 3 and compare with the pre-hibernation weight. Any significant loss requires a vet visit.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do all turtles hibernate?

No. Only temperate species brumate. Tropical species such as red-footed tortoises, sulcatas, and most softshells should be kept warm year-round. Cooling them is dangerous.

At what temperature do turtles hibernate?

Safe brumation is 4 to 10°C, with 5 to 8°C as the target. Below 4°C causes tissue damage. Above 12°C the metabolism rises and burns reserves too fast.

How long should a turtle hibernate?

8 to 16 weeks for most temperate species. Never brumate a first-year hatchling or a juvenile under 2 years — they lack the fat reserves to survive.

Can I hibernate my turtle in the fridge?

Yes. The fridge method is the safest option for most keepers. Use a fridge that never holds food, set it to 5 to 8°C, and check the box weekly.

What warning signs mean I should abort hibernation?

Wake the turtle if you see: weight loss above 10%, sunken eyes, soft or discolored shell, any nasal discharge, or regular movement suggesting the turtle is burning energy. Warm gradually — never rapidly.
