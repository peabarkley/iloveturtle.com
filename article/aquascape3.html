Undergravel vs. Bog Filters: Which Suits a Turtle Tank

2026-11-19

An undergravel filter uses the tank floor as its filter bed. A bog filter moves that bed into a separate planted area. Which suits your enclosure depends on where solids will settle, how much space you have and how easily you can clean the filter.

How undergravel filters work

A perforated plate draws water through the substrate. It saves space and uses the substrate as biological media, but food and feces settle underneath. Deep-cleaning a clogged bed can mean dismantling the tank. Avoid small loose gravel that a turtle could swallow.

How bog filters work

A pump sends water through a planted bed before it returns to the tank. Gravel surfaces support biological filtration and plants take up some nutrients as they grow. The bed takes room, and accumulated solids can clog it. Put a removable mechanical prefilter upstream.

Which setup suits your enclosure?

An undergravel plate takes up little room outside the tank. Choose it only if you can siphon the bed and reach the plate when flow falls. A bog bed suits a larger planted setup with space for plumbing and a prefilter that catches solids before water reaches the roots. If you are shopping for a standalone unit, see the turtle tank filter buying guide.

Designing a bog bed

Plan an overflow and a way to drain or flush the bed. Use turtle-safe plants suited to wet roots, and keep pump intakes and plumbing protected. Size the pump for its actual lift and keep return flow manageable for the turtle.

Improving an undergravel setup

If you already use a plate system, remove surface debris, check for uneven flow and consider reverse flow with a prefilter. If cleaning requires constant disassembly, a bare-bottom tank with an external filter may be easier.

How do you know the filter is working?

Test ammonia and nitrite, check for falling flow, and remove waste during regular partial water changes. Clear water alone does not show that the biological filter is keeping up.
